Nurettin Slide 3 — Dataset & Motivation
~65 sec
"Why the NBA? Because basketball is one of the few sports where every single play has been recorded and published since the very first season in 1947. Other sports have gaps in their historical data. The NBA does not.

Our main dataset comes from Basketball Reference — the gold standard for basketball statistics. It has 22 CSV files, 33 thousand rows, and 11 megabytes of data. It covers the NBA, the ABA, and the early BAA, all the way from 1947 to 2024.

We also used two extra datasets: one for player country, height, and weight from 1996 to 2023, and one for U.S. birth state data covering all of history.

We chose the NBA because the game has changed completely over 80 years. New rules changed strategy. Analytics changed how teams build rosters. More and more players came from other countries. Each of our five research questions looks at one part of this transformation."

Nurettin Slide 4 — RQ1: Three-Point Revolution
~65 sec
"RQ1 — how did the three-point shot change the game?

Before 1979, there was no three-point line in the NBA. Teams could only score 2 points on a field goal, no matter where they shot from. The ABA had a three-point rule before the NBA — the NBA finally added it in the 1979–80 season. At first, nobody used it much. In year one, teams took only 3.1 three-pointers per game.

Then something changed. In 1994, the league moved the line closer for three seasons — and you can see a spike in the data. Then in 2015–16, the Golden State Warriors used analytics to prove that a good three-pointer is worth more than a good mid-range two. Every team saw the numbers and changed how they played. Since then, the growth is almost vertical. By 2024, teams take 35.6 three-pointers per game — that is 11 times more than in 1980. Today, nearly 40% of all shots in the NBA are three-pointers."

Mehmet Arda Slide 5 — RQ1: Scoring & Pace Over Time
~35 sec
"Thanks Nurettin. This slide shows what happened to team scoring and pace at the same time as the three-point explosion.

In the 1990s and early 2000s, scoring was low — teams played slow, defensive basketball. Then it went up steadily to a record of about 113 points per game in 2024. Pace shows the same pattern: slow through the 90s, then faster again in the modern era. More three-pointers means more open space, which means more possessions and more scoring."

Mehmet Arda Slide 6 — RQ2: Physical Attributes vs Performance
~55 sec
"RQ2 — does height or weight predict how good a player is?

We used 12,844 player-seasons from 1996 to 2023, representing 82 countries. We compared height and weight against four performance stats: points per game, Win Shares, Player Efficiency Rating, and Box Plus/Minus. The scatter plot uses an OLS trendline to show the direction of the relationship.

The result for height vs. points: there is a small negative connection. Taller players score fewer points on average — not because they are worse, but because big players give value through rebounds and defense, not scoring. If you look at Win Shares instead, the trendline is almost flat. Height helps your team win, just in a different way than scoring.

Weight shows almost no connection with any stat. The NBA works for many different body types."
Go to Slide 7.
Mehmet Arda Slide 7 — RQ3: Career Arc & Peak Age
~50 sec
"RQ3 — at what age do players reach their best?

We used all player data from 1947 to 2024, keeping only seasons with at least 20 games played. Then we grouped all players by age and calculated the average stats. The result is a career arc — a line that shows how a typical NBA player develops.

Scoring peaks at age 26–27. Assists peak a little earlier, at 24–25. Rebounds peak around 25–26. These numbers are consistent across all eras.

After age 32, much fewer players are still active. After 35, only the very best players remain — so the average stats look a little high at older ages. This is called survivorship bias. We include a bar chart showing the number of players at each age, so you can see exactly where the data gets thin."

Q&A — 5 min

Possible questions. Who answers and what to say.

Q: "Why Streamlit? Why not Dash or a JavaScript framework?"

Nurettin

"Streamlit is the right fit for this project. It is pure Python, needs very little extra code, and starts with one command. Dash gives more layout control but requires much more code for the same result. A JS framework like D3 would be too complex — the course is about data storytelling, not front-end development. Streamlit let us focus on the analysis."

Q: "Why CSV files and not a database?"

Nurettin

"With 33 thousand rows and 11 megabytes, a database adds setup complexity with no performance benefit. Pandas reads the files in under one second, and @st.cache_data means it only happens once per session. For production with millions of rows and many users, a database would be right. For this project, flat files are simpler and easier to run — the instructor needs only one command."

Q: "Why team-level data for the three-point charts, not player-level?"

Nurettin

"This was actually a bug we found early on. If you average three-point attempts across all individual players, you get about 2 or 3 — because many big men almost never shoot threes. That makes the growth look very small. The real question is: how many threes does a team take per game? That went from 3.1 to 35.6. Team-level data tells the true story."

Q: "Is the trendline in Tab 2 statistically significant?"

Mehmet Arda

"Yes — the p-value for height vs. points is well below 0.05, so the negative slope is statistically significant. But the R-squared is only about 0.04 to 0.06. This means height explains only 4 to 6 percent of the difference in scoring between players. The connection is real, but height is not a good predictor of how many points a player will score."

Q: "Can you explain survivorship bias in more detail?"

Mehmet Arda

"At age 38, the only players still in the league are exceptional — they lasted because they are uniquely skilled. A typical player retires by 33 or 34. So the average stats at age 38 are higher than normal, because we are only seeing the very best — not a typical 38-year-old player. The bar chart in Tab 3 makes this clear: the sample size at age 38 is much smaller than at age 26."

Q: "The world map only goes to 2023. What about earlier international players?"

Yusuf

"Before 1996, international players were very rare. The NBA's globalization really started in the 1990s. Starting from 1996 captures the entire important period. For the U.S. state data, we use a different all-time dataset that covers the full history — that is the second map in Tab 4."

Q: "What would you add if you had more time?"

Yusuf

"Three things. First, a player search — type a name and see their personal career arc compared to the league average. Second, a salary dataset — how do advanced stats connect to contract values? Third, shot zone charts from the Player Shooting CSV, which is in our dataset but unused. A shot chart per player or era would be a natural next step."

Q: "How did you handle players who were traded mid-season?"

Mehmet Arda

"Basketball Reference shows traded players more than once — once for each team. For per-game stats, we use each row separately. For advanced stats like Win Shares, we group by player and season — adding totals and averaging rates — so a traded player gets one combined number per season. The join with the physical dataset uses lowercase player name matching to handle small formatting differences."
